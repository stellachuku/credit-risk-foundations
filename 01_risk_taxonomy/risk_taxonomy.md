The major types of financial risk and, more importantly, how they interact.

1.	CREDIT RISK:
a)	What is it: Credit risk is the risk of financial loss because a borrower or counterparty fails to meet its contractual payment obligations.
b)	What causes it: Credit risk arises when a borrower’s ability or willingness to pay deteriorates. Main causes include: Weak financial performance: falling revenue, profits or cash flow. High debt: too much debt relative to the company's ability to service it. Liquidity problems: insufficient cash to meet obligations when due. Business or industry deterioration: loss of customers, recession, falling commodity prices, increased competition. Company-specific problems: poor management, governance or business decisions. Concentration: excessive dependence on one customer, supplier, market or sector. CFA Institute identifies macroeconomic conditions, financing mismatches and issuer-specific factors as key sources of credit risk.
c)	What does a credit analyst look for: When assessing credit risks, A credit analyst is essentially asking:"Can this borrower repay us, and how much could we lose if they don't?". They also look at: Business: How does the company make money and what could disrupt it? Financials: Revenue, profitability, liquidity, leverage and cash flow. Repayment capacity: Where will the money to repay the debt come from? Debt burden: How much debt does the company already have? Downside risk: What happens if the business performs worse than expected? Protection/recovery: What collateral, guarantees or other protection exists? CFA Institute specifically identifies business model, industry, competitive position, governance, profitability, liquidity, leverage and coverage as important credit-analysis factors.
d)	Give one business example: Example: A fuel distributor requests a €10 million credit facility. The credit analyst would ask: Can the company generate enough cash to repay €10 million? They examine its financial statements, cash flow, existing debt, customers and industry.
They discover that: the company is profitable, cash flow is currently adequate, but 35% of its sales come from one customer and that customer has started paying late. The analyst therefore sees increased credit risk because a problem with one major customer could reduce the company's cash flow and its ability to pay its own creditors.
The analyst would then assess:
Probability of default: How likely is the company to fail to pay?
Exposure at default (EAD): How much would be outstanding if it defaults?
Loss given default (LGD): How much might the lender actually lose after recoveries?
These are core credit-risk components in the Basel framework. 
The one-line version to remember: Credit risk = the risk that someone who owes you money does not pay, resulting in a financial loss. Credit analysis = assessing whether they can pay, what could stop them from paying, and how much you could lose if they don't.

2.	MARKET RISK:
Market risk is the risk of loss caused by movements in market prices or market variables. For banks, Basel identifies the main market-risk factors as interest rates, FX, equity prices and commodity prices. Basel explicitly identifies interest-rate, FX, equity and commodity risk as the core market-risk factor categories.
a)	Interest rates: Interest-rate risk arises because changes in interest rates affect the value of financial instruments and/or the cost of funding. For fixed-rate bonds, the basic relationship is:
Interest rates ↑ → bond prices ↓
Interest rates ↓ → bond prices ↑
CFA identifies price risk and reinvestment risk as important components of interest-rate risk.
For a corporate borrower, higher interest rates can also increase its interest expense if its debt is floating-rate or needs to be refinanced.
b)	FX: FX risk is the risk that changes in exchange rates cause financial loss. For example: A company has a USD 10 million receivable but reports in EUR. If the USD depreciates against the EUR before the receivable is collected, the EUR value of that receivable falls. FX exposure can therefore affect both the market value of a position and the cash flow of a company. CFA specifically notes that the return on a foreign asset depends on both the asset's return and the change in the exchange rate.
c)	Commodity prices: Commodity risk is the risk that changes in commodity prices cause losses.
This is particularly important for businesses involved in oil, gas, metals, agriculture and other physical commodities. For example:CA trader agrees to purchase oil at $80/barrel and later the market price falls to $65. If the trader is exposed to the price decline, the position loses value. Basel notes that commodity markets can be more volatile and less liquid than interest-rate and FX markets, and identifies spot-price, basis, interest-rate and forward-price risks within commodity exposures.
d)	Equity prices: Equity price risk is the risk of loss caused by changes in the value of shares or equity-related positions. For example: A bank holds €20 million of listed shares. The market falls 15%. The position loses approximately €3 million in market value, before considering hedges or other effects. CFA identifies equity prices as one of the principal sources of market risk. 

How market risk can eventually become credit risk? 
Market risk can create or increase credit risk when a market movement weakens a borrower's or counterparty's ability to meet its financial obligations. The chain is:
Market shock → financial deterioration → weaker cash flow/net worth/collateral → higher probability of default → credit risk

Example 1: Interest rates → credit risk
A company has €100 million of floating-rate debt. Interest rates rise significantly.
→ Interest expense increases → EBITDA-to-interest coverage deteriorates → Cash flow available for debt service falls → Debt service becomes harder → Probability of default increases.
The original shock is market risk. The resulting possibility that the company cannot repay its debt is credit risk.

Example 2: Commodity prices → credit risk
Consider an oil producer.Oil price falls sharply.
→ Revenue falls → EBITDA falls → Cash flow deteriorates → Leverage increases → Debt-service capacity weakens → Probability of default increases.
For the bank that lent to the oil producer, the original commodity-price movement is a market event, but the deterioration in the borrower's ability to repay is credit risk.
This is particularly important in credit analysis because the market variable can be an important driver of the obligor's PD and potentially LGD.

Example 3: FX → credit risk
A company generates revenue in EUR but has substantial USD debt. EUR depreciates against USD.
→ EUR cost of servicing the USD debt increases → Cash flow available for debt service decreases → Leverage and debt-service metrics deteriorate → Creditworthiness deteriorates
→ Default risk increases
Again, the FX movement itself is market risk. The resulting deterioration in repayment capacity is credit risk.

Example 4: Equity/asset prices → credit risk
Suppose a company has borrowed against securities or other assets. The market value of the collateral falls.
→ Collateral coverage deteriorates → Loan-to-value increases
→ The lender may require additional collateral → If the borrower cannot provide it, the lender's exposure increases → Credit risk increases

There is therefore a very important interaction between market risk and credit risk.

3.	LIQUIDITY RISK: 
Liquidity risk is the risk that a company cannot obtain enough cash, when needed, to meet
its financial obligations.
For a non-financial company, that could mean being unable to pay suppliers, employees,
taxes, interest, or loan principal when they fall due.
Basel defines funding liquidity risk as the risk that a firm cannot meet expected and unexpected current and future cash-flow and collateral needs without harming its operations or financial condition. 
In simple terms: Liquidity risk = “Will I have the cash when I need to pay?”
a)	What does liquidity actually mean: Liquidity means having cash, or assets that can be converted into cash quickly enough and at a reasonable cost, to meet obligations. Cash is the most liquid asset. Receivables, inventory and other assets may have value, but they are not equivalent to cash because they may take time to convert. The ECB describes liquidity as the ease and speed with which an asset can be converted into cash or used to settle a liability. So, when analysing a company, don't simply ask: Does the company have assets?” Ask:“What can actually be turned into cash, how quickly, and at what cost?” That distinction is critical.
b)	Why can a profitable company still have liquidity problems? Because profit is an accounting measure; liquidity is a cash-flow and timing issue. A company can report a profit but have little cash available. For example: It makes EUR10m of sales. Customers have 90-day payment terms. The company must pay suppliers in 30 days. It therefore records revenue and potentially profit today, but the cash arrives later. The company can be profitable but temporarily short of cash. The same problem can arise when cash is tied up in inventory, receivables or other assets. CFA specifically distinguishes profitability from liquidity and emphasises cash-flow analysis when assessing a company's ability to meet fixed obligations. The fundamental distinction: Profit asks: "Did the business make money?" Liquidity asks: "Does the business have enough cash at the right time?"
c)	Why does this matter to a lender? Because a lender gets repaid with cash, not accounting profit. A borrower may be profitable and have strong assets, but if it cannot generate or access cash when debt service is due, it can still default.For a lender, liquidity therefore affects the borrower's ability to service debt: Can it pay interest? Can it repay principal when due? Are receivables converting into cash? Is too much cash tied up in inventory? Does it depend heavily on short-term borrowing? Does it have sufficient cash or committed facilities to withstand a period of weaker cash flow? CFA identifies liquidity, profitability, leverage and coverage as important measures when assessing a corporate borrower's ability to meet fixed debt obligations. The lender's perspective in one sentence - Profitability tells me whether the business is economically viable; liquidity tells me whether it can actually pay me when the payment is due.
“One important correction to a common credit-analysis mistake: don't treat current assets minus current liabilities or a current ratio as proof that a company is liquid. Liquidity is fundamentally about cash-flow timing, cash availability, funding access and the quality/convertibility of assets, not just the size of the balance sheet. Basel explicitly requires forward-looking cash-flow analysis across different time horizons”.

4.	OPERATIONAL RISK:
The core definition is consistent across the regulatory sources: operational risk is the risk of loss arising from failed or inadequate internal processes, people and systems, or from external events. Basel and the ECB also explicitly include legal risk within operational risk.
a)	People: Losses caused by human error, inadequate skills, poor management, misconduct or excessive reliance on key employees. Example is An employee enters the wrong payment amount or an analyst makes a material processing error. It can affect financial performance / creditworthiness through, Financial losses, compensation costs, delays and potentially regulatory or legal costs. Repeated problems can weaken profitability and management confidence. GARP specifically identifies staff turnover, poor management, inadequate training and key-person dependency as people-risk sources.
b)	Processes: Risk that internal procedures are poorly designed, incomplete, unclear or not followed. Example is when a credit approval process fails to identify a required covenant or collateral condition. It can affect financial performance / creditworthiness through Errors, losses, delayed transactions, control failures and potentially regulatory penalties. Poor processes can also make the business inefficient and reduce profitability.
c)	Systems: Risk that IT, technology, data or other systems fail, are unavailable, inaccurate or compromised. Example is when A bank's payment system goes down or incorrect data feeds into a credit system. It can affect financial performance / creditworthiness through, Business interruption, lost revenue, remediation costs, customer compensation and potentially significant losses. The ECB specifically includes ICT risk within operational risk.
•	Fraud: Intentional misconduct by employees or external parties that causes loss. Example is Employee theft, manipulation of financial information, hacking or payment fraud. It can affect financial performance / creditworthiness through, Direct financial loss, investigation and remediation costs, legal/regulatory consequences and potentially damage to the firm's reputation. Basel identifies both internal and external fraud as operational-risk event types.
•	External events: Events outside the firm's direct control that disrupt operations. Example is Cyberattack, natural disaster, pandemic, fire or major disruption at a critical third party. It can affect financial performance / creditworthiness through, Operations may stop, revenue may fall, costs may increase and the firm may struggle to meet obligations. Basel's operational-resilience guidance specifically highlights cyber incidents, technology failures and natural disasters.

“Operational problems can eventually affect financial performance and creditworthiness as follows:

Operational risk does not automatically equal credit risk. But a material operational failure can create or worsen financial risk, including credit risk.
The chain can be: Operational failure → financial loss / business disruption → lower earnings and cash flow → weaker liquidity/capital → reduced ability to meet obligations → weaker creditworthiness.
For example: A company's payment-processing system fails → customers cannot transact → sales fall → cash inflows decline → liquidity becomes tight → suppliers are paid late → lenders see deterioration in liquidity and repayment capacity.
That is why operational risk matters in a credit assessment. You are not necessarily asking, "Does this company have operational risk?" Every company does.
You are asking: "Could an operational failure be sufficiently severe to damage this company's cash flow, liquidity, capital position or ability to service debt?"
That is the more useful credit-risk question. The CFA Institute also makes the broader point that risks interact and that non-financial risks can ultimately affect an organisation's value and financial position.

5.	COUNTERPARTY RISK: 
a)	What a counterparty is: A counterparty is simply the other party involved in a financial transaction. For example: Bank lends to Company A ‚Üí Company A is the bank's counterparty.  Company A buys oil from Company B on 30-day terms ‚Üí Company B is the counterparty to Company A. Two banks enter an interest-rate swap ‚Üí each bank is the other's counterparty. The ECB defines a counterparty as the opposite party in a financial transaction. Simple definition to remember: Counterparty = the party on the other side of your transaction.
b)	How counterparty risk differs from traditional borrower/credit risk: Traditional borrower credit risk is usually unilateral. Counterparty credit risk can be bilateral and can change over time. Traditional borrower credit risk. Suppose a bank lends €10 million to a company. The bank is exposed to the company defaulting. The exposure is relatively straightforward: the company owes the bank money. CFA Institute describes credit risk as the risk of economic loss resulting from a borrower failing to make required payments. 
Counterparty credit risk:
Now consider an interest-rate swap or derivatives transaction. The value of that transaction can move as interest rates, FX rates, commodity prices, etc. change.

Today: Company A may owe Bank B €2m.
Tomorrow: Bank B may owe Company A €3m.
Therefore, the exposure is not fixed and can change during the life of the transaction.

Basel defines CCR as the risk that a counterparty defaults before final settlement, where the transaction has positive economic value to the party exposed. Basel specifically distinguishes this from ordinary lending, where the bank's credit exposure is generally unilateral. Basel also describes CCR as a multidimensional risk because exposure and counterparty credit quality can both change, while market movements can affect the exposure.

The key distinction:
	Traditional borrower risk	Counterparty credit risk
1	Usually a loan/receivable	Often derivatives, securities financing, long-settlement transactions, etc.
2	Exposure is mainly to the borrower	Exposure can change with market values
3	Generally unilateral	Can be bilateral
4	Focus heavily on borrower ability to repay	Must consider counterparty default and future transaction exposure

c)	Why it matters particularly in transactions involving multiple parties?
Because one party's failure can affect several other parties, creating interconnected exposures and potentially larger losses.

Consider: Bank A → Counterparty B → Counterparty C → Bank D
If B defaults, the consequences may not stop with the transaction between A and B. Other parties may also have exposures to B, need to replace transactions, liquidate collateral, or meet their own obligations.
This is particularly important in financial markets because institutions can have many transactions with the same counterparty and many counterparties simultaneously.
Basel explicitly highlights the correlation between exposures among a bank's counterparties and the interaction between counterparty credit risk and market risk.
The ECB also explains why central counterparties exist: they can replace exposures to multiple trading counterparties with exposure to a single central counterparty, helping manage interconnected counterparty exposures.

The important point is:  It is not simply "more parties = more risk."

The real issue is:
Multiple parties create interconnected exposures, so the default of one counterparty can affect the obligations, liquidity and exposures of others. 
That is why CCR management looks beyond simply asking "Is this counterparty creditworthy?

It also asks:
How much could we be exposed to? How could that exposure change?
What happens if the counterparty defaults? What collateral and netting arrangements exist?
What other exposures do we have to the same counterparty?
Could market movements make the exposure substantially larger?
Basel specifically requires banks' CCR frameworks to consider credit, market, liquidity, legal and operational risks and their interrelationships.

If you need to remember only three sentences, it must be:

Counterparty: the other party on the other side of a financial transaction.
Counterparty risk: the risk that that party fails to fulfil its obligation, particularly before a transaction is finally settled; unlike ordinary lending, the exposure can change with market movements.
Multiple parties: interconnected transactions can transmit losses and create concentration and systemic effects if one counterparty defaults.

These are the formulations to be used in a credit/risk interview, because they stay close to the Basel and ECB definitions rather than using the looser way "counterparty risk" is sometimes used in commercial credit discussions.
