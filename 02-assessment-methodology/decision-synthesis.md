# Stella Chuku Finance
# Decision Synthesis — Version 1.0

**Parent framework:** Capital & Business Decision Architecture V1.0  
**Parent methodology:** Business Assessment Methodology V1.0  
**Status:** Initial framework; subject to case validation  
**Date:** August 2026

---

# 1. Purpose

Decision Synthesis converts the findings from the business assessment into a clear capital-provider decision.

It answers:

> **Given the business thesis, dominant risks, mitigations, residual risk, risk appetite and economics, what should the capital provider do?**

---

# 2. Core Principle

> **The five domains should not be averaged into a single score.**

A business can have:

- strong economics but unacceptable refinancing risk;
- weak resilience but exceptional returns;
- excellent management but structurally poor economics;
- strong fundamentals but an unacceptable valuation.

The decision must therefore be driven by the issues that actually determine the outcome.

---

# 3. Decision Synthesis Chain

The decision follows:

**Business Thesis**

↓

**Value Drivers**

↓

**Dominant Threats**

↓

**Materiality**

↓

**Mitigation / Structure**

↓

**Residual Risk**

↓

**Risk Appetite / Capacity**

↓

**Risk-Adjusted Economics**

↓

**Decision**

↓

**Conditions / Remediation**

↓

**Monitoring**

---

# 4. Step 1 — State the Business Thesis

Summarise:

> **Why does this business create value?**

Identify the critical assumptions supporting the thesis.

Examples:

- sustainable pricing power;
- customer retention;
- market growth;
- cost advantage;
- scalable operating model;
- attractive incremental returns.

The thesis becomes the reference point against which risks are assessed.

---

# 5. Step 2 — Identify the Dominant Value Drivers

Identify the factors that create most of the business's economic value.

Examples:

- market position;
- pricing power;
- customer stickiness;
- cost advantage;
- recurring revenue;
- high incremental ROIC;
- asset utilisation.

The assessment should focus on whether these drivers are durable.

---

# 6. Step 3 — Identify the Dominant Threats

Identify the limited number of findings capable of changing the decision.

Typical examples:

- customer concentration;
- refinancing dependence;
- declining incremental ROIC;
- weak cash conversion;
- excessive leverage;
- weak management credibility;
- competitive deterioration;
- unrealistic growth assumptions.

Do not allow minor issues to obscure the dominant threats.

---

# 7. Step 4 — Assess Materiality

Apply the Materiality Framework.

For each dominant finding determine:

- economic consequence;
- business-thesis impact;
- mitigability;
- risk appetite / policy impact;
- classification.

Classify as:

- Decision-Critical
- Material
- Relevant
- Informational

Only Decision-Critical and Material findings should normally drive the final decision.

---

# 8. Step 5 — Assess Mitigation

For each material issue:

> **What can realistically reduce, protect against, restructure or price the risk?**

Assess:

- effectiveness;
- evidence;
- enforceability;
- timing;
- management execution;
- cost.

---

# 9. Step 6 — Determine Residual Risk

For each material risk establish:

**Initial Risk → Mitigation → Residual Risk**

The key question is:

> **Is the residual risk acceptable for this capital provider?**

---

# 10. Step 7 — Apply Risk Appetite and Capacity

Assess:

### Risk Appetite

Is the residual risk within the provider's willingness to accept risk?

### Risk Capacity

Can the provider actually absorb the exposure?

### Hard Constraints

Are there policy, regulatory, mandate or exposure restrictions?

A transaction cannot proceed simply because the economics appear attractive if a hard constraint is breached.

---

# 11. Step 8 — Assess Risk-Adjusted Economics

Determine whether the economics compensate for the residual risk.

## Lender

> **Does expected contractual return adequately compensate for probability and severity of loss?**

Consider:

- pricing;
- fees;
- exposure;
- tenor;
- security;
- expected loss.

## Investor

> **Does expected equity return adequately compensate for investment risk at the proposed valuation?**

Consider:

- entry valuation;
- growth;
- ROIC;
- downside;
- exit;
- required return.

## Acquirer

> **Does expected post-acquisition value justify the purchase price and transaction risk?**

Consider:

- maintainable earnings;
- purchase price;
- synergies;
- integration costs;
- capital requirements;
- downside.

---

# 12. Decision Blockers

A Decision-Critical finding may become a blocker when it cannot reasonably be:

- mitigated;
- structured;
- priced;
- accepted within appetite.

Examples:

### Lender

Fundamental inability to service debt under credible downside conditions.

### Investor

Expected return insufficient to compensate for residual risk.

### Acquirer

Maintainable earnings cannot be established or transaction value cannot justify the purchase price.

---

# 13. Decision Outcomes

## Fundability

### Fundable

Risk is acceptable and economics are adequate.

### Fundable with Conditions

Risk is acceptable subject to specified actions or protections.

### Fundable Only at Revised Terms

The business may be acceptable, but not at the proposed:

- amount;
- tenor;
- pricing;
- structure;
- security.

### Not Currently Fundable

Residual risk or economics are unacceptable.

---

## Investability

### Investable

Risk-adjusted return is attractive.

### Investable with Conditions

Investment is acceptable subject to defined conditions.

### Investable Only at Revised Valuation / Terms

Business is attractive but current economics do not adequately compensate for risk.

### Not Currently Investable

Risk-adjusted return is inadequate.

---

## Acquisition Readiness

### Due-Diligence Ready

Financial, commercial and operational evidence is sufficiently robust for transaction diligence.

### Ready with Remediation

Material issues can be addressed before or through the transaction.

### Material Diligence Gaps

Important evidence or uncertainties remain unresolved.

### Not Currently Transaction-Ready

The risks, evidence gaps or economics are not sufficiently acceptable.

---

# 14. Decision Response

The decision should specify the appropriate action.

Possible responses:

**Accept**

Proceed.

**Condition**

Proceed subject to defined requirements.

**Structure**

Change the transaction to reduce risk.

**Price**

Adjust pricing, valuation or required return.

**Reduce**

Reduce exposure or capital committed.

**Remediate**

Require business improvements before proceeding.

**Decline**

Do not proceed.

---

# 15. Decision Statement

Every assessment should end with a concise decision statement.

Recommended structure:

> **Decision:** [Outcome]

> **Why:** [Business thesis + dominant value drivers]

> **Principal concerns:** [2–5 dominant threats]

> **Residual risk:** [Assessment after mitigation]

> **Economics:** [Whether return/value compensates for residual risk]

> **Conditions:** [Required actions / protections]

> **Monitoring:** [Key assumptions and triggers]

---

# 16. Example

### Business

Strong market position and attractive margins.

### Dominant strengths

- customer stickiness;
- cost advantage;
- strong management;
- high historical ROIC.

### Dominant threats

- 40% EBITDA customer concentration;
- refinancing requirement;
- declining incremental ROIC.

### Mitigation

- long-term customer contract;
- refinancing plan;
- reduced growth capex.

### Residual risk

Moderate.

### Lender conclusion

**Fundable with conditions**

- lower initial exposure;
- shorter tenor;
- minimum liquidity;
- refinancing milestone.

### Investor conclusion

Potentially investable, but:

- lower valuation;
- higher return requirement;
- capital deployment linked to ROIC milestones.

### Acquirer conclusion

Potentially acquirable, subject to:

- customer diligence;
- valuation adjustment;
- refinancing before closing.

The same business can therefore produce different decisions under different lenses.

---

# 17. CEO-Level Output

The decision synthesis should ultimately identify:

> **The 3–5 things management needs to understand about its business before seeking capital or a buyer.**

These should be the findings most likely to affect:

- funding;
- investment;
- valuation;
- transactionability;
- resilience;
- future value.

This is a core client-facing output of Stella Chuku Finance.

---

# 18. Monitoring Link

Every material decision should identify the assumptions that could invalidate it.

Structure:

**Decision Assumption → Indicator → Threshold → Trigger → Action**

Example:

**Assumption:** Customer concentration remains manageable.

**Indicator:** Largest customer as % of EBITDA.

**Trigger:** Material contract deterioration.

**Action:** Reassess exposure / valuation / transaction risk.

---

# 19. What the Framework Does Not Do

The decision synthesis does not:

- average domain scores;
- allow strengths to automatically offset decision blockers;
- treat all risks equally;
- assume mitigation is effective without evidence;
- replace professional judgment;
- determine risk appetite itself.

It integrates the outputs of the assessment into a decision.

---

# 20. Core Principles

### Principle 1
> **The decision is driven by dominant issues, not the number of issues.**

### Principle 2
> **The five domains are not averaged.**

### Principle 3
> **Residual risk matters more than gross risk.**

### Principle 4
> **Risk appetite and capacity determine whether residual risk is acceptable.**

### Principle 5
> **Risk-adjusted economics determine whether accepting that risk is worthwhile.**

### Principle 6
> **A good business is not automatically a good lending, investment or acquisition opportunity.**

### Principle 7
> **A material risk does not automatically require rejection.**

### Principle 8
> **Conditions, structure and pricing can change the decision.**

### Principle 9
> **Decision assumptions must become monitoring triggers.**
