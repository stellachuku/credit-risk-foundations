# Capital & Business Decision Architecture — Version 1.0

**Status:** Architecture defined; assessment methodology under development  
**Date:** 15 August 2026

---

## 1. Purpose

The Capital & Business Decision Architecture is the core decision framework of Stella Chuku Finance.

It helps businesses understand how sophisticated external decision-makers evaluate them before committing capital or acquiring the business.

The architecture translates the perspective of lenders, investors and acquirers into a structured business assessment.

It is designed to answer three business-readiness questions:

### FUNDABLE

Would a lender provide capital? If yes:
- How much?
- For how long?
- At what price?
- Under what structure and protections?

### INVESTABLE

Would an investor provide capital? If yes:
- What risks would they take?
- What returns would they require?
- What value-creation opportunities would they see?
- At what valuation and terms?

### ACQUISITION-READY

Would a buyer acquire the business?

If yes:
- Would the financial and operational story survive due diligence?
- Are the earnings sustainable and transferable?
- What risks could reduce value?
- What would affect valuation or transaction structure?

---

## 2. Core Proposition

The architecture is based on the premise that sophisticated capital providers are not simply asking whether a business is profitable.

They are trying to determine:

> **How durable is the economic value of this business, what could impair that value, can management be trusted to protect and grow it, how does the business behave under stress, and does the risk-adjusted outcome justify the capital or transaction commitment?**

The architecture therefore moves beyond conventional financial health analysis.

---

## 3. The Five Analytical Domains

The underlying business is assessed through five domains:

1. **Economic Value & Durability**
2. **Dependency & Fragility**
3. **Management, Governance & Credibility**
4. **Financial & Operational Resilience**
5. **Capital Productivity & Financial Architecture**

These domains are complementary.

They are not five independent scores and should not automatically be equally weighted.

Their purpose is to expose the underlying economics, vulnerabilities, management quality, resilience and capital structure of the business.

---

## 4. The Architecture Logic

The architecture follows this analytical sequence:

**VALUE**

↓

**DEPENDENCY**

↓

**MANAGEMENT**

↓

**RESILIENCE**

↓

**CAPITAL**

↓

**RISK / RETURN / APPETITE**

↓

**DECISION**

↓

**MONITORING**

### What each stage asks

**Value**
> Does the business create attractive and durable economic value?

**Dependency**
> What must remain true for that value to persist?

**Management**
> Can management and shareholders be trusted to protect and execute the business?

**Resilience**
> What happens when the business's assumptions are wrong or external conditions deteriorate?

**Capital**
> Is capital being used productively and is the business funded appropriately?

**Risk / Return / Appetite**
> Is the residual risk acceptable and adequately compensated for this particular capital provider?

**Decision**
> Should the capital provider fund, invest, acquire, restructure, condition or decline?

**Monitoring**
> What evidence would indicate that the original decision thesis is becoming wrong?

---

## 5. The Five Domains Are Not the Evidence Categories

Financial, commercial, operational and governance information are sources of evidence.

The five domains are the analytical framework used to interpret that evidence.

### Evidence sources

- Financial
- Commercial
- Operational
- Management / Governance
- Strategic

### Decision domains

- Economic Value & Durability
- Dependency & Fragility
- Management, Governance & Credibility
- Financial & Operational Resilience
- Capital Productivity & Financial Architecture

Therefore:

**Evidence → Analysis → Findings → Five Domains → Decision**

## 6. The Universal Analytical Chain

Every material finding should move through the following chain:

**Evidence**

What do we know?

↓

**Analysis**

What does the evidence show?

↓

**Finding**

What is actually happening?

↓

**Interpretation**

Why does it matter economically?

↓

**Domain**

Which decision domain does it affect?

↓

**Materiality**

How much can it affect the decision?

↓

**Mitigation / Structure**

Can the issue be reduced, protected, priced or structured?

↓

**Residual Risk**

What risk remains after mitigation?

↓

**Capital-Provider Implication**

What does the residual risk mean for the lender, investor or acquirer?

↓

**Decision**

What should the capital provider do?

↓

**Monitoring Trigger**

What future evidence would cause the conclusion to change?

## 7. Risk Appetite Overlay

Risk appetite is not a standalone domain.

It is an overlay applied after the business risks, value drivers and mitigants have been assessed.

The architecture distinguishes four concepts:

### Risk

What could go wrong?

### Risk Appetite

How much risk is the capital provider willing to accept?

### Risk Capacity

How much risk can the capital provider actually take?

### Risk-Adjusted Return

Is the compensation adequate for taking that risk?

A business can therefore be:

- economically attractive but outside a particular capital provider's appetite;
- risky but acceptable if appropriately structured and compensated;
- financially strong but unsuitable because the expected return is inadequate;
- weak in one area but acceptable because the risk is effectively mitigated.

## 8. Decision Lenses

The same business evidence can lead to different decisions depending on the capital provider.

### Lender

Primary objective:

> Protect repayment while achieving an acceptable risk-adjusted return.

Primary decision:

> **Fundable?**

The lender focuses particularly on:
- cash-flow durability;
- debt-service capacity;
- liquidity;
- leverage;
- refinancing;
- downside resilience;
- security;
- covenants;
- expected loss;
- risk appetite.

---

### Investor

Primary objective:

> Generate an attractive risk-adjusted return and preserve upside while controlling downside.

Primary decision:

> **Investable?**

The investor focuses particularly on:
- economic durability;
- market opportunity;
- competitive advantage;
- growth quality;
- incremental ROIC;
- management;
- capital allocation;
- valuation;
- downside;
- exit potential.

---

### Acquirer

Primary objective:

> Acquire durable economic value at a price and structure that adequately compensate for transaction and ownership risks.

Primary decision:

> **Acquisition-ready?**

The acquirer focuses particularly on:
- maintainable earnings;
- quality of earnings;
- commercial durability;
- operational transferability;
- management dependency;
- customer concentration;
- working capital;
- net debt;
- valuation;
- transaction protections;
- post-acquisition capital requirements.

---

## 9. Materiality

Materiality is decision-specific.

A finding is potentially material when it can:

1. Affect repayment, investment return, valuation or transaction value;
2. Be insufficiently mitigated;
3. Breach risk appetite, policy or capacity;
4. Undermine a fundamental business thesis.

Materiality categories:

### Decision-Critical

Could independently change the decision or fundamentally undermine the business thesis.

### Material

Meaningfully affects risk, return, valuation, structure or conditions.

### Relevant

Important to monitor but does not currently change the decision.

### Informational

Useful context without meaningful decision impact.

---

## 10. Decision Synthesis

The architecture does not use a simple average of the five domains.

The final decision is formed by:

**Business Thesis**

↓

**Dominant Value Drivers**

↓

**Dominant Threats**

↓

**Mitigation / Structural Protection**

↓

**Residual Risk**

↓

**Risk Appetite / Capacity**

↓

**Risk-Adjusted Return / Value**

↓

**Decision**

The assessment should identify the few issues that actually determine the decision rather than treating every finding as equally important.

---

## 11. Decision Blockers

A decision blocker is a finding that cannot reasonably be offset by strengths elsewhere.

Examples:

### Lender

Fundamental inability to service debt under credible downside conditions.

### Investor

Expected return insufficient to compensate for residual risk.

### Acquirer

Inability to establish maintainable earnings or fundamental inability to justify the transaction valuation.

Decision blockers must be assessed in the context of the relevant capital provider.

---

## 12. Decision Outputs

The architecture produces three primary outputs.

### Fundability

Possible conclusions:

- Fundable
- Fundable with conditions
- Fundable only at revised terms
- Not currently fundable

Output:

**Amount → Tenor → Pricing → Structure → Security → Covenants → Conditions**

---

### Investability

Possible conclusions:

- Investable
- Investable with conditions
- Investable only at revised valuation / terms
- Not currently investable

Output:

**Value-Creation Thesis → Expected Return → Key Risks → Downside → Valuation / Terms**

---

### Acquisition Readiness

Possible conclusions:

- Due-diligence ready
- Ready with remediation
- Material diligence gaps
- Not currently transaction-ready

Output:

**Value Drivers → Diligence Risks → Value Leakage → Remediation → Valuation / Transaction Impact**

---

## 13. Monitoring

Monitoring is designed to test the assumptions behind the original decision.

It is not simply periodic financial reporting.

The monitoring structure is:

**Assumption → Indicator → Threshold → Trigger → Action**

Example:

**Assumption:** Pricing power remains intact.

**Indicator:** Gross margin.

**Threshold:** Defined minimum margin.

**Trigger:** Persistent deterioration beyond threshold.

**Action:** Reassess economic durability and capital exposure.

---

## 14. CEO Blind-Spot Output

A key client-facing output of the architecture is:

> **The 5 Things External Capital Providers or Buyers May See That Management Should Know**

The purpose is to identify issues that may not be obvious from headline financial performance.

Examples:

- Growth is consuming more capital than it creates.
- Revenue appears diversified but cash flow is highly concentrated.
- Forecast growth depends on assumptions unsupported by historical execution.
- The business can survive a shock but loses too much strategic flexibility.
- The funding structure is inconsistent with the duration and volatility of cash flows.

---

## 15. What the Architecture Is Not

The architecture is not:

- a generic financial-health checklist;
- a credit-rating model;
- a financial model;
- a due-diligence checklist;
- a lender-only framework;
- an investor-only framework;
- a mechanical scoring model;
- a substitute for professional judgment.

The architecture is the **decision framework**.

Financial analysis, commercial analysis, operational analysis, case studies, scoring tools and client diagnostics are built on top of it.
