# Decision Lenses — Version 1.0

**Parent framework:** Capital & Business Decision Architecture V1.0  
**Status:** Defined; subject to case validation  
**Date:** August 2026


# 1. Purpose

The Capital & Business Decision Architecture assesses the underlying business through five analytical domains.

However, the same business does not produce the same decision for every capital provider.

A lender, investor and acquirer have different:
- objectives;
- downside exposures;
- time horizons;
- return requirements;
- risk tolerances;
- sources of value;
- protections available to them;
- decision constraints.

The **Decision Lenses** translate the same underlying business assessment into the appropriate decision for each capital provider.

The core principle is:

> **The evidence may be the same. The decision is not necessarily the same.**



# 2. The Three Decision Lenses

The architecture uses three primary decision lenses:

1. **Lender — Fundability**
2. **Investor — Investability**
3. **Acquirer — Acquisition Readiness**

Each lens has its own decision objective and ultimate decision criterion.



# 3. LENDER LENS — FUNDABILITY

## Ultimate Decision Criterion

> **Will the risk-adjusted return justify the probability and severity of loss, within the lender's risk appetite and capacity?**

The lender's primary concern is not whether the business is attractive in an absolute sense.

The question is:
> **Can the business repay the lender under the expected case and credible downside scenarios, and is the lender adequately compensated and protected for the residual risk?**



## 3.1 Primary Objective

Protect repayment while achieving an acceptable risk-adjusted return.

The lender therefore focuses heavily on:
- cash-flow durability;
- debt-service capacity;
- liquidity;
- leverage;
- refinancing risk;
- resilience;
- security;
- covenants;
- concentration;
- expected loss;
- risk appetite;
- exposure limits.



## 3.2 How the Five Domains Are Viewed by a Lender

### Economic Value & Durability
Primary question:

> **Does the business generate sufficiently stable cash flow to support debt repayment?**

The lender is particularly interested in:
- recurring earnings;
- cash conversion;
- working-capital requirements;
- margin stability;
- capital intensity;
- competitive durability.

High accounting profitability without reliable cash generation is insufficient.



### Dependency & Fragility

Primary question:
> **What could cause cash flow or repayment capacity to deteriorate?**

Focus areas:
- customer concentration;
- supplier dependence;
- key-person dependence;
- refinancing;
- shareholder support;
- pricing pressure;
- market exposure.



### Management, Governance & Credibility

Primary question:
> **Can management be relied upon to execute the business plan, identify problems early and take corrective action?**

Evidence includes:
- forecast accuracy;
- historical execution;
- transparency;
- financial discipline;
- response to setbacks;
- shareholder commitment.



### Financial & Operational Resilience

Primary question:

> **Can the business continue servicing debt when conditions deteriorate?**

Focus:
- liquidity;
- capitalization;
- stress cash flow;
- covenant headroom;
- recovery capacity;
- additional funding requirements.



### Capital Productivity & Financial Architecture
Primary question:

> **Is the business generating enough cash and is its capital structure compatible with debt service?**

Focus:
- leverage;
- debt maturity;
- refinancing;
- debt-service burden;
- funding structure;
- capital intensity;
- shareholder distributions.



## 3.3 Lender Risk-Return Trade-off

A lender generally has:
- capped upside;
- meaningful downside;
- contractual repayment;
- defined maturity;
- limited participation in enterprise-value upside.

Therefore, the lender is less willing than an equity investor to rely on future upside to compensate for weak repayment capacity.

The lender must be satisfied that:

> **The downside is sufficiently controlled and the contractual return adequately compensates for the residual credit risk.**



## 3.4 Typical Lender Responses

Where risk is manageable but elevated:
- reduce exposure;
- shorten tenor;
- increase pricing;
- strengthen covenants;
- require additional liquidity;
- require security;
- require guarantees;
- require shareholder support;
- impose information requirements;
- condition funding on remediation.

Where risk cannot be adequately mitigated:

> **Decline / do not fund.**



## 3.5 Fundability Outputs

The assessment should produce:

### Decision

- Fundable
- Fundable with conditions
- Fundable only at revised terms
- Not currently fundable

### Required output

> **Amount → Tenor → Pricing → Structure → Security → Covenants → Conditions**



# 4. INVESTOR LENS — INVESTABILITY

## Ultimate Decision Criterion

> **Will the risk-adjusted return justify the capital at the proposed valuation, within the investor's risk appetite and capacity?**
The investor is not primarily asking whether the business can repay capital.

The investor is asking:
> **Can this business create sufficient future value to justify the price paid and the risks assumed?**



## 4.1 Primary Objective

Generate an attractive risk-adjusted return while controlling downside and preserving upside.

The investor therefore focuses heavily on:
- economic value creation;
- market opportunity;
- competitive advantage;
- growth quality;
- incremental ROIC;
- management;
- capital allocation;
- valuation;
- downside;
- exit potential.



## 4.2 How the Five Domains Are Viewed by an Investor

### Economic Value & Durability

Primary question:
> **Can the business generate attractive returns on capital over time?**

Focus:
- ROIC;
- incremental ROIC;
- margin durability;
- pricing power;
- market position;
- growth economics;
- cash generation.



### Dependency & Fragility

Primary question:

> **What could impair future enterprise value or the investment thesis?**

Focus:
- customer concentration;
- market dependence;
- key-person dependence;
- supplier dependence;
- competitive pressure;
- funding dependence.



### Management, Governance & Credibility

Primary question:

> **Can management convert the business opportunity into the forecast value creation?**

Focus:
- strategic execution;
- forecast credibility;
- capital allocation;
- adaptability;
- governance;
- shareholder alignment.



### Financial & Operational Resilience

Primary question:

> **What happens to enterprise value when the investment thesis is stressed?**

Focus:
- downside scenarios;
- liquidity;
- capitalization;
- recovery;
- additional capital requirements;
- ability to continue investing through stress.



### Capital Productivity & Financial Architecture

Primary question:

> **Will incremental capital generate sufficient returns, and is the existing capital structure supporting rather than destroying value?**

Focus:
- incremental ROIC;
- capital allocation;
- leverage;
- refinancing;
- distributions;
- recapitalisation requirements.



## 4.3 Investor Risk-Return Trade-off

An investor has:

- potentially significant upside;
- potentially significant downside;
- greater exposure to enterprise-value changes;
- longer investment horizons;
- greater dependence on management execution.

Therefore:
> **A high-risk business can still be investable if the expected return and valuation adequately compensate for the risk.**

Conversely:
> **A high-quality business can be uninvestable if the entry valuation leaves insufficient return for the risk being assumed.**

This is a critical distinction.

**Business quality ≠ investment attractiveness.**



## 4.4 Typical Investor Responses

Where the business is attractive but risks remain:

- invest at a lower valuation;
- require a higher expected return;
- stage the investment;
- require recapitalisation;
- require governance rights;
- require performance milestones;
- structure downside protection;
- require management changes or commitments.

Where the return does not compensate for residual risk:

> **Do not invest.**



## 4.5 Investability Outputs

### Decision

- Investable
- Investable with conditions
- Investable only at revised valuation / terms
- Not currently investable

### Required output

> **Value-Creation Thesis → Expected Return → Key Risks → Downside → Valuation → Terms**



# 5. ACQUIRER LENS — ACQUISITION READINESS

## Ultimate Decision Criterion

> **Will the risk-adjusted value justify the purchase price and transaction risk, within the buyer's strategic and financial constraints?**

An acquirer is fundamentally different from both a lender and an investor.

The acquirer is purchasing the underlying business and therefore inherits:
- operating risks;
- employees;
- customers;
- suppliers;
- systems;
- liabilities;
- management dependencies;
- capital requirements;
- execution risks.

The central question is therefore:

> **What economic value will the buyer actually acquire, and what could cause that value to be lower than expected after closing?**



## 5.1 Primary Objective

Acquire durable economic value at a price and structure that adequately compensate for transaction and ownership risks.

The acquirer therefore focuses heavily on:
- maintainable earnings;
- quality of earnings;
- commercial durability;
- operational transferability;
- management dependency;
- customer concentration;
- working capital;
- net debt;
- capital requirements;
- valuation;
- transaction protections.



## 5.2 How the Five Domains Are Viewed by an Acquirer

### Economic Value & Durability

Primary question:
> **What earnings and cash flows are genuinely maintainable after acquisition?**

Focus:
- normalized EBITDA;
- recurring revenue;
- margins;
- pricing;
- customer retention;
- competitive position;
- capital intensity.



### Dependency & Fragility

Primary question:
> **What dependencies will the buyer inherit?**

Focus:
- customer concentration;
- supplier concentration;
- founder/key-person dependence;
- contracts;
- funding;
- operational dependencies.



### Management, Governance & Credibility

Primary question:
> **Can the business continue to perform after ownership changes?**

Focus:
- management dependency;
- succession;
- governance;
- execution;
- incentives;
- strategic alignment.



### Financial & Operational Resilience

Primary question:
> **How much financial and operational risk will the buyer inherit after closing?**

Focus:
- liquidity;
- working capital;
- debt;
- operational resilience;
- capital requirements;
- downside scenarios.



### Capital Productivity & Financial Architecture

Primary question:
> **Will the acquired business generate adequate returns on the buyer's invested capital, and is its existing funding structure appropriate?**

Focus:
- ROIC;
- incremental returns;
- debt;
- refinancing;
- required recapitalisation;
- future capex;
- working capital.



## 5.3 Acquirer Risk-Return Trade-off

An acquirer must consider:
- purchase price;
- expected future cash flows;
- integration costs;
- transaction costs;
- required investment;
- downside;
- strategic value;
- financing structure;
- ability to extract or create synergies.

Therefore:
> **A strong business can still be an unattractive acquisition if the purchase price captures too much of the expected value.**

Conversely:
> **A business with material weaknesses may still be acquirable if those weaknesses are understood, priced correctly and adequately protected against.**



## 5.4 Typical Acquirer Responses

Where the business is fundamentally attractive but risks remain:
- reduce valuation;
- restructure debt;
- condition closing on remediation;
- require management transition;
- use earn-outs;
- use contingent consideration;
- require warranties and indemnities;
- impose transaction conditions;
- require seller financing;
- retain key management.

Where the risks cannot be appropriately priced or protected:

> **Do not acquire.**



## 5.5 Acquisition-Readiness Outputs

### Decision

- Due-diligence ready
- Ready with remediation
- Material diligence gaps
- Not currently transaction-ready

### Required output

> **Value Drivers → Diligence Risks → Value Leakage → Remediation → Valuation / Transaction Impact**



# 6. SAME EVIDENCE, DIFFERENT DECISION

The decision lenses are designed to prevent the assumption that one business assessment produces one universal answer.

### Example: Customer concentration

A customer represents 40% of EBITDA.

### Lender asks:

> Could loss of this customer impair debt repayment?

Potential response:
- lower exposure;
- shorter tenor;
- stronger covenant;
- liquidity requirement.

### Investor asks:

> Does this concentration undermine the durability of future enterprise value?

Potential response:
- lower valuation;
- higher required return;
- diversification milestone.

### Acquirer asks:

> Is the 40% EBITDA contribution genuinely maintainable after acquisition?

Potential response:
- deeper customer diligence;
- valuation adjustment;
- retention protections;
- transaction conditions.

The underlying evidence is the same.

The decision consequence differs.



# 7. COMPARATIVE DECISION LOGIC

| Dimension | Lender | Investor | Acquirer |
|||||
| Primary objective | Protect repayment + earn return | Create value + earn return | Acquire durable value |
| Core question | Can debt be repaid? | Can equity value grow sufficiently? | What value am I actually buying? |
| Main downside | Default / loss | Loss of invested capital / value | Overpaying / inherited risks |
| Main upside | Contractual return | Enterprise-value appreciation | Business ownership + synergies |
| Time horizon | Contractual maturity | Investment horizon | Long-term ownership |
| Key financial focus | Cash flow / debt service | ROIC / FCF / growth | Maintainable earnings / FCF |
| Key risk focus | Credit risk | Investment risk | Transaction + ownership risk |
| Main pricing mechanism | Interest / fees | Entry valuation | Purchase price |
| Main protection | Security / covenants | Governance / investment terms | Warranties / indemnities / structure |
| Typical risk response | Reduce exposure / tighten terms | Lower valuation / higher return | Lower price / transaction protection |
| Ultimate criterion | Risk-adjusted return vs loss risk | Risk-adjusted return vs valuation | Risk-adjusted value vs purchase price |



# 8. DECISION LENS PRINCIPLE

The architecture therefore does **not** ask:

> "Is this a good business?"

It asks:
> **"Is this a sufficiently attractive risk-adjusted opportunity for this specific capital provider, given the price, structure, protection and constraints available?"**

This distinction is fundamental.

A business can simultaneously be:
- **Fundable but not Investable**
- **Investable but not Fundable**
- **Investable but not Acquisition-Ready**
- **Fundable and Investable but unattractive at the proposed acquisition price**
- **Acquirable after valuation adjustment but not financeable on conventional debt terms**

There is no requirement for the three conclusions to be identical.



# 9. DECISION CONDITIONS

A positive decision does not necessarily mean "no risks."

The decision may instead be:

> **Accept the opportunity subject to specified conditions that reduce the residual risk to an acceptable level.**

Conditions should be linked to identified risks.

Examples:

### Lender

Risk:
> Refinancing dependence.

Condition:
> Extend maturities and maintain minimum liquidity.

### Investor

Risk:
> Declining incremental ROIC.

Condition:
> Investment staged against defined return milestones.

### Acquirer

Risk:
> Key-person dependency.

Condition:
> Management retention and succession arrangements before/at closing.



# 10. DECISION-PROVIDER BLIND SPOTS

Each capital provider should also ask:

> **What might I be underestimating because of the way I view the business?**

### Lender blind spot
Over-reliance on collateral or historical repayment while the underlying business economics deteriorate.

### Investor blind spot
Overweighting growth potential while underestimating capital intensity and execution risk.

### Acquirer blind spot
Overestimating synergies or maintainable earnings while underestimating inherited operational dependencies.

These blind spots should be tested during assessment.



# 11. FINAL DECISION STRUCTURE

For each decision lens, the final assessment should state:

### 1. Decision
What should the capital provider do?

### 2. Why
What are the dominant value drivers and risks?

### 3. Residual Risk
What remains after mitigation?

### 4. Economics
Is the expected return/value sufficient?

### 5. Conditions
What must be true before or after the transaction?

### 6. Monitoring
What evidence would cause the decision to be revisited?



# 12. CORE PRINCIPLE

> **The architecture assesses the business.**
> **The decision lens determines how that assessment matters to the specific capital provider.**
> **Risk appetite determines what level of residual risk is acceptable.**
> **Risk-adjusted economics determine whether accepting that risk is worthwhile.**

Together, these produce the final decision
